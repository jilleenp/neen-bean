Witch's Walk

by Jilleen Phillips
published on September 4, 2026

Note: On September 12, the Historical Society of Pownal, Vermont, will hold its forth annual "Witch's Walk," in honor of Margaret Schumacher Krigger. Margaret holds two important honors for me: she was reportedly the only woman in the state of Vermont ever tried as a witch, and she was my fifth-great grandmother. I am very proud ot that.

I will tell the witchcraft story another time. What is most important is the "exceptional" woman that she was.

This is the speech that I wrote to be shared at that event.

William was given six sheep, one axe, and two hymn books in the "Dutch language." Little John received six sheep, a large English Bible, and a silver pocket watch. But sixteen-year-old granddaughter Margaret was given, among other things: silk garments, a looking glass, a tea set with six cups and saucers. And, in the words of her grandmother, young Margaret received a special gift: "one feather bed Which I usually lye on.” Margaret wasn't simply leaving her possessions. She was giving a young woman the tools to establish a respectable household and a place in the community.

Young Margaret received one more very important thing from her grandmother: an indomitable spirit.

By the time she penned her will in 1790 at the age of sixty-four, Margaret Schumacher Krigger knew well how hard life could be. She had survived the French and Indian wars, the American Revolution, and the "border wars" between New York and New Hampshire. She had built a home, a family and a thriving mill that stood at the center of her community. She had lost her husband and two sons. And according to history books, she had even been tried for and acquitted of witchcraft.

Yes, Margaret knew well how difficult it was to build a life on the frontier, so she gave her granddaughter what she believed she would need to build one. And then she signed her Last Will and Testament with a strong, bold "M."

She died just five days later.

The Krigger family and their neighbors - about fifteen families of Dutch and German descent - settled in North Pownal along the banks of the Hoosic River, near where we are standing today. The great rocks still known as Krigger's Rock overshadow the turbulent waters of the river that were the heart-beat of the community. The river powered the mill built by Margaret and her family, a mill that was said to be so efficient that people would walk for miles to bring their goods to be ground.

The North Pownal community was forced from their farms at least three times by bands of waring French and Indians throughout the 1740s and 1750s. Margaret saw her farm burned, livestock killed, friends and neighbors ambushed and scalped, and she and her family fled to nearby Fort Massachusetts for safety.

The Kriggers were nearly evicted again in August of 1764, this time by men they considered neighbors. It is said that two Williamstown land speculators drove Johannes and Margaret Krigger from the farm they had lived on for about thirty years. These men claimed the land under a grant from New Hampshire. Johannes believed that he had held the land legally for nearly thirty years under an earlier grant from New York. Johannes was required to pay forty-five dollars to retrieve the livestock that the speculators had driven off as a way of evicting the family. The Kriggers eventually left their farm in about 1767 to establish a thriving mill in nearby Williamstown, Massachusetts. Meanwhile, the "border war" disputes between the Commonwealths of New York and New Hampshire sizzled for another twenty-seven years until the State of Vermont was established in 1791.

One story about Margaret's trial for witchcraft, apparently first written down in 1868 by T. E. Brownell, says that that Margaret Krigger's neighbors considered her "exceptional." What could make her neighbors say that?

At a time when women's rights were severely limited by law and even more so by societal expectation, Margaret surely stood out. The few records we have of the Krigger family suggest that Margaret was of an independent mind and a strong spirit.

When Johannes and Margaret's youngest son, Peter, died in 1772, he intentionally chose to leave a portion of his share of the family business to Margaret, calling her ‘My Honored Mother.’ Those are not the words I would expect from a son describing a woman who simply stood in the background of the family business. While it was legal for a woman to have a share in a business, it was certainly not standard practice. It suggests that Margaret had a considerable influence over the mill—and perhaps that her influence extended beyond her household into the wider community.

Margaret lost her second son, William, in the Battle of Bennington in 1777, and took over the care and raising of his three children. In 1785, after Johannes died and Margaret was legally considered a "femme sole," Margaret used a portion of her inheritance to purchase property that had been seized from William's estate. She immediately transferred it to her three minor grandchildren, ensuring for them a secure future. Margaret was not able to give the children their father back, but she could build them a home.

Yes, Margaret knew exactly how difficult life could be. But the items that she intentionally chose to leave behind give us a glimpse into the life she had worked so hard to build for her family. She bequeathed silk and linen garments, a tea set, a looking glass. These are the symbols of a woman who had carefully protected a way of life for herself and her family through wars and the hardship of living in a rural 18th century community.

Her sixteen-year-old granddaughter, Margaret - who received the clothing and household goods - married Abiel Cook just months after her grandmother's death. Her grandmother's legacy helped her to build a new home for herself and her family.

In about 1801, Margaret and her husband Abiel, sold the property her grandmother had given her. She packed up her family of seven children, all under the age of ten, and left the only home she had ever known. They moved about 200 miles away to settle in the New York frontier of Otsego county.

We may never know what really happened that frigid day so long ago, when Margaret was plunged beneath the ice on suspicion of witchcraft. But I do know this: her blood runs in my veins, and I hear her voice whisper to me down through the years. I am proud to be the great-granddaughter of Margaret Schumacher Krigger, and I hope that I, too, have inherited her indomitable spirit.

Jilleen Walch Phillips
